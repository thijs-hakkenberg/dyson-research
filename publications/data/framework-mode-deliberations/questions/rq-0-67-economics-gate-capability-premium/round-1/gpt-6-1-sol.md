## 1. Frame & decision type

**Recommendation: an above-parity capability premium requires a separate, explicit owner decision. D-01 does not itself authorise one.** That decision may occur at the same KDP-0B meeting, but it must not be recorded as an economics-gate pass.

The question is one of **decision authority and gate semantics**, not merely the value of a multiplier. D-01 establishes the purpose of Phase 0 and preserves the option to undertake 0B; it does not specify how much additional expenditure capability is worth.

This is an executive policy decision governing a substantially irreversible commitment. Under D-02, its evidence and authorisation requirements should therefore be stronger than those for reversible internal 0A milestones.

The distinction to preserve is:

- **Economics finding:** does the proposed supply route satisfy the approved cost criterion?
- **Investment decision:** is the capability nevertheless worth purchasing at an explicitly bounded premium?

The owner may answer “no” to the first and “yes” to the second without contradiction.

## 3. Options

**A. Null: retain the unresolved panel split.**  
Argument for: preserves negotiating flexibility while 0A generates evidence.  
Reject: analysts cannot design a discriminating economics test against three incompatible thresholds. It invites selection of the favourable interpretation at KDP-0B.

**B. Permit an owner-set premium inside the economics gate.**  
Use Claude’s proposed \(P70_{\text{project}}\le R\,P50_{\text{Earth}}\), with \(R>1\) permitted.  
Argument for: integrates capability valuation and cost into one decision.  
Reject as the default: D-01 supplies neither a value for \(R\) nor a premium budget. If \(R\) is independently justified, funded and approved beforehand, this becomes substantively close to the separate decision recommended below—but retains a less informative “economics pass” label.

**C. Require cost parity or better, with no premium route.**  
Argument for: simple, fiscally disciplined; a strict undercut variant follows Gemini’s position.  
Reject: prevents the owner from purchasing valuable capability even where its benefits exceed its additional cost. Strict undercutting also requires a justified margin, rather than an arbitrary requirement to beat parity by any amount.

**D. Separate the economics finding from capability-premium authorisation. — Recommended**  
Apply a stable parity test. An above-parity project fails that test, but may proceed through a separately recorded, bounded owner authorisation. Coordinate both decisions in one gate package.

## 5. Sensitivity vs trade-off points

### Sensitivity points — empirical or modelling questions

1. **Delivered quantity and utilisation.** Signed Phase 1 demand should size 0B, as D-01 requires. Nameplate production must not be treated as saleable or accepted delivery.
2. **Launch-price assumptions.** Earth resupply and the project’s own launch requirements must use coherent scenarios; both respond to launch prices.
3. **Grade, recovery, replacement and delay.** These affect both expenditure and accepted kilograms at SEL1. Lost output and replacement launches cannot be evaluated independently if failures correlate them.
4. **Comparator definition.** Earth supply must deliver equivalent material quality, availability and schedule—not merely launch an equivalent mass.
5. **Cost scope.** Include development still to be incurred, transport, operations, losses, replacement, closure and contingent liabilities on a consistent basis.

Scenario reversals are information, not automatically modelling errors. Require a **non-contingent economics pass** to survive the predeclared core scenario set. If parity holds only under certain launch prices or demand levels, the finding should be conditional, with enforceable triggers, or require premium authorisation.

### Trade-off points — owner values, not facts to discover

- **Capability versus expenditure:** how much additional public or programme funding is worth autonomy, learning or strategic supply?
- **Risk versus early commitment:** how much downside is acceptable to secure an earlier capability?
- **Decision speed versus assurance:** how much independent review is justified before an irreversible commitment?

An owner can resolve these trade-offs; improved cost modelling cannot resolve them on the owner’s behalf.

## 8. Decision, warrant, accepted downside

### Proposed rule text

> **g-economics — KDP-0B**
>
> 1. Compare risk-adjusted expected delivered cost per accepted kilogram at SEL1 against the equivalent Earth-supply counterfactual, using a common price base, demand profile, service specification and declared evaluation horizon.
> 2. For the forward investment decision, compare all remaining avoidable costs consistently; disclose previously incurred programme costs separately. Do not compare a project marginal cost with an Earth full-service cost.
> 3. An ordinary economics pass requires expected project cost no greater than expected Earth-supply cost, plus compliance with an owner-approved P70 funding envelope and downside limits fixed before gate adjudication.
> 4. Report absolute funding exposure, cost-ratio uncertainty and results across predeclared launch-price, demand, utilisation, replacement and delay scenarios. P70 project cost divided by P50 Earth cost may be reported, but is not itself the probability of beating Earth supply.
> 5. Above-parity expenditure is **not an economics pass**. It requires a separate owner decision specifying the capability purchased, beneficiaries, alternatives, maximum incremental premium, total funding envelope, downside acceptance and stop/review triggers.
> 6. Without that authorisation, an economics failure invokes the agreed fallback: **0A only, Earth supply**. Premium approval does not waive any other gate, including D-02’s no-waiver TRL requirement.

For an ordinary pass, the premium allowance is effectively **\(R=1\)**. If a premium is authorised, report its implied \(R\), but do not treat that ratio as a substitute for an absolute funding cap. A falling Earth benchmark can increase \(R\) without increasing the project budget; the two measures answer different questions.

**Warrant:** capability value can justify investment without demonstrating cost competitiveness. Keeping those propositions separate prevents a strategic rationale from silently changing a cost threshold.

**Accepted downside:** an above-parity project with genuine strategic merit will carry an explicit adverse economics finding. That may be politically uncomfortable and creates additional approval work. The discomfort is useful disclosure, not a defect to remove by relabelling the gate.
